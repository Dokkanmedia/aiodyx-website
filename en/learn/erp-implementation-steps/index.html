ERP implementation, step by step

How an ERP project runs from analysis to post-go-live support, what your company must prepare, the most common mistakes, and realistic timelines.

The 30-second summary
  1. A successful implementation goes through seven stages, starting with how each department works.
  2. Prepare your data and an owner per department before starting; those cause most delays.
  3. Going live in phases is less risky than switching everything on in one day.

Implementing an ERP is a change in how the company works, and the software is one part of it. Success depends on clear processes, data quality and the commitment of the internal team as much as on the system. These are the stages most projects go through, with what the company must provide at each one.

01

The seven stages

  1. Analysis: the partner meets each department to understand current processes, pain points and required reports. The output is a requirements document and a clear scope of what is in the project and what is deferred.
  2. Design: processes are mapped to the standard system. Settings are defined, gaps that need development are listed, and access rights and approval flows are drawn up.
  3. Configuration and development: the system is set up per the design, and agreed extensions and integrations are built, such as e-invoicing, banks and biometric devices.
  4. Data migration: master data such as customers, vendors, products and employees is loaded, then opening balances and open transactions.
  5. Testing: the company's users test full end-to-end processes on their own data and sign off on them.
  6. Training: each user is trained on their actual tasks, and key users are trained to become the internal reference.
  7. Go-live and support: work moves to the system on a set date, followed by a period of close support to handle issues and fine-tune.
02

What the company must prepare

  • A decision-maker from management who settles disputes between departments and approves the scope.
  • An internal project manager who tracks tasks and dates with the partner.
  • A key user from each department with time to take part in analysis and testing.
  • Clean data: customer, vendor and product lists without duplicates, and reconciled balances.
  • Samples of the current reports and documents the system must produce.
  • Accounts and access for the government platforms and banks the integrations need.
03

Common mistakes

  • Copying the old way as is: many changes to mimic the previous system raise cost and make upgrades harder. Start from the standard process and change it only where there is a real need.
  • Uncontrolled scope growth: every new request mid-project delays go-live. Log new requests for a later phase.
  • Leaving data cleanup for later: bad data moves into the new system and destroys trust in it from day one.
  • Shallow testing: checking separate screens instead of a full cycle from order to journal entry.
  • Late or generic training: users need training on their own tasks, close to go-live.
  • Going live during a busy period close: pick a date that allows a clean close and clear opening balances.
04

Realistic timelines

Duration depends on the number of apps, companies and branches, data volume, the number of integrations and custom developments, and how fast the company makes decisions. As a rough range:

  • A small company with a few standard apps: from several weeks to about three months.
  • A mid-sized company with several departments and government integrations: roughly three to six months.
  • A group of companies, complex manufacturing or heavy development: six months or more, often in phases.

These are general market ranges. Treat them as a guide only. A partner who gives an exact duration before analysis is guessing. Ask for a timeline after the analysis stage.

05

Going live in phases

In large projects, a phased go-live reduces risk. For example: accounting, sales, purchase and inventory first, then HR and payroll, then manufacturing or projects. Each phase settles before the next, and the team learns from each launch.

06

Conclusion

A successful ERP project rests on a clear scope, clean data and an internal team with enough time. The partner brings system knowledge, and the company brings business knowledge. FAQ

Should we migrate all historical data?

Usually not. Projects typically move master data, opening balances and open transactions, and keep the old archive for reference. The decision depends on your need for comparative reports.

Should we run both systems in parallel for a while?

Parallel running doubles the team's workload. Thorough testing before go-live followed by a full switch works better, with the old system kept read-only.

How much of our team's time does the project need?

Key users need regular time every week, and more during testing and training. Plan this with their managers from the start.
